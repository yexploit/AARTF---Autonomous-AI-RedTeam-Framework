Chapter 1

Introduction to anomaly and trend detection

Why do some words suddenly dominate social media overnight, while others disappear silently?

Text does not stay still. A brand, a campus, or a country can talk about the same themes for months, and then the vocabulary flips in a few hours. Module 4 is about noticing that flip, deciding whether it is a rare shock or a lasting shift, and choosing the right visual or statistical tool to prove it.

1.1 What is an anomaly in text mining?

Definition

An anomaly in text mining is a textual pattern or behaviour that deviates significantly from normal or expected language use — over time or across groups. In plain language: something rare, unexpected, or abnormal in the text stream.

Anomalies can point to rare events, abnormal user behaviour, or emerging threats.

Everyday analogy

Think of a classroom attendance register. Students attend regularly. One day, 90 percent are absent. That day is an anomaly. In text, a sudden change in language behaviour plays the same role.

Examples

  • A sudden spike of words such as scam, fraud, fake in banking emails.
  • A single chat message using extreme violent language in a normally polite forum.
  • A gaming chat that suddenly fills with abusive language — a cyberbullying signal.
  • Unusual medical terms appearing in health forums — a possible outbreak signal.
Classroom scenario

You monitor Twitter posts about a popular smartphone brand. For months the usual words are battery, camera, performance. Suddenly thousands of posts contain overheating within a few hours. Is that a normal wobble, or something unusual? That is the starting question of anomaly and trend detection.

1.2 What is trend detection?

Definition

Trend detection identifies gradual or sudden changes in topics, keywords, sentiments, or discussion patterns. A trend is what is slowly or rapidly becoming popular and then staying in the conversation.

Trends help us read evolving public opinion, emerging issues, and long-term behavioural shifts. The key contrast with an anomaly is duration and stability:

IdeaCharacterTypical shape in time
AnomalyRare and unexpectedSharp spike, then either dies or transforms
TrendSustained and evolvingRise that continues, plateaus, or slowly decays

1.3 Why this matters in the real world

Anomaly and trend detection are used wherever language is an early warning system:

Fraud detection Public opinion monitoring Health surveillance Cybersecurity Misinformation tracking Online safety

What an anomaly can indicate:

  • Rare events
  • Abnormal user behaviour
  • Cyber threats
  • Emerging social issues
  • Coordinated misinformation campaigns

1.4 Three types of anomalies in text

TypeMeaningLecture example
Point anomaly A single document or message that is abnormal by itself. One extremely aggressive comment in a polite discussion group.
Contextual anomaly A document is anomalous only in a given time or situation. Earthquake is normal after a tremor; the same word flooding ordinary daily news may be anomalous.
Collective anomaly A group of documents is abnormal together. Hundreds of similar fake reviews posted within minutes. Each review can look ordinary; the swarm is the anomaly.

Many important public events start as a linguistic shock and then settle into a new normal. The lecture diagram “From Anomaly to Trend Evolution” makes this visible: a sudden word explosion on the left, then a continuous discussion on the right as new vocabulary becomes everyday language.

Solved case study A

Sudden change in social media conversations

Problem as given in the lecture

During the final week of March, students collect Twitter posts about daily life in India. For several weeks the most common words are movies, exams, travel, cricket, weekend.

On one particular day they notice a sudden, sharp increase — within a few hours — of lockdown, curfew, COVID, quarantine, essential services.

In the following weeks, new words appear frequently and consistently: work from home, online classes, vaccination, booster dose.

Analyse this behaviour using text mining techniques.

Question 1 · Identify the text anomaly and justify

The anomaly is the single-day explosion of lockdown, curfew, COVID, quarantine, essential services. Those words were not part of the baseline vocabulary of movies, exams, travel, cricket, and weekend. The change is sudden (hours, not weeks), large (a sharp increase), and unexpected against the historical distribution. That is a classic contextual / temporal anomaly: the words are meaningful in a crisis, but they are abnormal relative to ordinary daily-life talk. If many near-duplicate posts arrive together, the same burst is also a collective anomaly.

Question 2 · How the anomaly turns into a trend

After the shock, the conversation does not snap back to cricket and weekends. New terms — work from home, online classes, vaccination, booster dose — keep appearing frequently and consistently. Frequency-over-time plots would show a spike that does not die; topic models would show a new theme (public health / remote life) replacing or sitting beside entertainment. That sustained, evolving vocabulary is a trend. In lecture language: the overnight word explosion (anomaly) becomes continuous discussion (trend). Topic drift also happens inside the health theme: hospitals → vaccines → booster dose.

Question 3 · Why sudden word explosions matter

A word explosion is often the first public trace of a real-world shock — a lockdown, a product failure, a scam, an outbreak, or a coordinated campaign. Detecting it early lets journalists, governments, platforms, and brands react before the event is officially confirmed. Missing it means you discover the crisis only after it has already become the new baseline.

Question 4 · Two real-world domains

Any two well-justified domains from the module are acceptable. Strong answers:

  1. Public-health surveillance — unusual symptom or hospital words in forums can flag an outbreak.
  2. Cybersecurity / online safety — sudden abusive language or grooming phrases can flag cyberbullying or predation.
  3. Finance and fraud — spikes of scam / fraud / fake in mail or reviews.
  4. Brand and product monitoring — the smartphone overheating burst.
  5. Misinformation tracking — coordinated identical posts appearing in minutes.
Question 5 · Which visualization, and why

The best primary view is a term-frequency line graph (or stream graph) over time for the baseline words versus the crisis words. A line graph shows the exact moment of the spike and whether the new words stay high — which is exactly the anomaly-to-trend story. A word cloud is a weak choice: it hides time and rare-but-critical terms. A heat map of topics by week is a good companion view for “health dominates after March.” Visual analytics (click the spike, read the tweets) is the right analyst workflow.

Chapter 2

Text visualization techniques for pattern discovery

How can we “see” patterns hidden inside thousands of documents?

2.1 Why visualization is necessary

Text is high-dimensional, unstructured, and hard to read as a table of word counts. A journalist cannot read 10,000 Indian newspaper headlines by hand, and a spreadsheet of frequencies still hides what is emerging or declining. Visualization turns those numbers into shapes the eye can judge in seconds.

Visualization in text mining is used to:

  • identify dominant themes
  • detect sudden changes or anomalies
  • understand sentiment flow
  • compare topics over time
Key advantage

Humans are better at pattern recognition visually than through raw numbers.

2.2 Word clouds — seeing what dominates

In a word cloud, size is proportional to frequency. An election-tweet cloud might show large vote, leader, rally and smaller economy, education. That is a fast overview of what people talk about most.

Best forNot suitable for
Quick overview, exploratory analysis, presentations Exact comparison, detecting rare events
Limitation

Word clouds are visual summaries, not precise tools. A cloud can hide rare hate-speech terms that are critical.

2.3 Term-frequency plots — numbers with clarity

Bar charts of exact counts beat clouds when you need comparison. Typical e-commerce complaint bars: delivery delay, damaged product, refund issue. The lecture slide pairs this idea with TF–IDF style ranking charts (before versus after an event) so you can see which terms actually rose, not just which words look big.

2.4 Term frequency over time

Words change meaning and importance. The frequency of lockdown from 2019 to 2021 is near zero before 2020, spikes in 2020, then declines. That single series is already both an anomaly detector and a trend detector.

The lecture chart overlays several terms (for example education, politics, vaccine, boycott) so an election peak and a later scandal peak are visible on the same timeline.

2.5 Line graphs and area plots

Use these for the rise and fall of keywords, sentiment, and topic popularity.

Example — movie reviews: positive sentiment rises after release, then drops when negative reviews arrive. Stacked area plots add a second gift: they show the share of positive / neutral / negative at each time, not only one line.

2.6 Network graphs — relationships in text

Nodes are words or entities. Edges are relationships or co-occurrence. In a cyberbullying analysis, the target’s name sits in the centre and abusive terms (loser, hate, attack, dumb) connect to it. Thick edges mean frequent pairing; thin edges mean weaker pairing. A second, messier network of many accounts can reveal a coordinated pile-on rather than one bully.

Figure 2. A star-shaped co-occurrence graph: abusive vocabulary attached to one target.

2.7 Topics, heat maps, and stream graphs

Topic models produce themes such as:

  • Topic 1: education, exams, results
  • Topic 2: health, hospital, vaccine

A heat map shows topic strength across documents or months. Politics darkens in election months; health darkens during a pandemic. Darker colour means stronger intensity.

A stream graph (or topic timeline) shows how topics flow: emergence, peak dominance, gradual decline. Online education emerges during lockdown, peaks during exams, then stabilises.

2.8 Visual analytics — humans in the loop

Visual analytics combines automated text mining with interactive interfaces. The analyst does not stop at the chart: they click a spike and read the actual messages that caused it. That human step is what turns a pretty graph into a decision.

2.9 Limitations and domain knowledge

  • Visualization can oversimplify complex data.
  • Minority patterns may be hidden by dominant words.
  • Poor design can mislead.

The same picture means different things to a data scientist, a journalist, and a cybersecurity analyst. Correct interpretation always depends on context.

Solved exercise B

Spot the pattern before it becomes a trend

Problem as given in the lecture

You are given one month of Indian social-media posts about daily life, entertainment, education, and public events.

First three weeks — most frequent words: movies, exams, travel, cricket, weekend.

Fourth week — a sudden spike in leak, boycott, scam.

Over the next few days, new words appear consistently: investigation, apology, legal action.

Question 1 · Which words are the anomaly, and why?

leak, boycott, scam are the anomaly. They break a three-week baseline of ordinary lifestyle talk, they arrive as a sudden spike (not a slow climb), and they are semantically unexpected in that corpus. That is a temporal point/contextual anomaly at the vocabulary level.

Question 2 · Are investigation and legal action an anomaly or a trend?

They represent the start of a trend (or the evolution of the anomaly into a trend). The lecture stresses that an anomaly is rare and unexpected, while a trend is sustained and evolving. These words appear consistently over the next few days, which is duration, not a one-hour burst. They also show narrative development: leak/scam → investigation → apology → legal action. If they later vanish after one afternoon, you could revise the label; on the evidence given, they are a trend.

Question 3 · Word cloud or line graph for the spike?

Choose the line graph. A cloud only says a word is large; it cannot show that the size appeared in week 4 rather than week 1. A line (frequency versus day) makes the sudden spike unmistakable and lets you compare leak / boycott / scam with the old baseline series. That is why the lecture says clouds are not suitable for detecting rare events or exact comparison.

Question 4 · Hundreds of nearly identical posts in minutes

This is a collective anomaly. Each post may look like a normal complaint or opinion. Together, the volume, timing, and near-duplicate wording are abnormal — the same logic as hundreds of similar fake reviews posted within minutes.

Question 5 · What action should an analyst recommend?

Do not wait for a trained classifier. First, verify the spike (sample posts, check duplication, bots, and whether a real leak/scam is being reported). Then escalate to the relevant desk — newsroom, platform trust-and-safety, campus exam cell, or brand legal — with a time-series chart and a few representative messages. Monitor the follow-on terms (investigation, apology, legal action) so the team can see whether the event is becoming a lasting controversy. If the swarm is coordinated abuse, apply the cyberbullying playbook from Chapter 6 rather than treating it as ordinary chatter.

Chapter 3

Data exploration and the search for novel patterns

How do analysts find unknown unknowns in massive text before they build models?

3.1 Exploratory text data analysis (ETDA)

Most text-mining failures come from poor data understanding, hidden bias, or overfitting to dominant patterns. Exploratory analysis is the pre-modelling validation layer.

Definition

ETDA is the quantitative and qualitative examination of a corpus to study statistical distributions, linguistic structure, and temporal behaviour. Its output is not a prediction. Its output is a set of hypotheses about the data.

3.2 Document-length distribution

Measure tokens per document and the shape (skew) of that distribution.

  • Very long documents can dominate TF–IDF vectors.
  • Extremely short texts lose semantic context.
  • A spike in ultra-short news headlines often means breaking-news alerts.

3.3 Vocabulary richness and lexical diversity

Key metrics

Vocabulary size |V| = number of unique word types.

TTR = |V| / (total tokens)

Hapax legomena = words that appear only once.

High lexical diversity may indicate

  • opinionated or creative content
  • emerging narratives

Low diversity may indicate

  • bot-generated or copied text
  • template spam and coordinated campaigns

3.4 Frequency distribution and Zipf’s law

Word frequencies in natural language follow Zipf’s law: a few words are very frequent, most words are rare. That matters because the rare tail often carries the novel or critical signal — lockdown, evacuation, cyberattack start rare and later become important.

3.5 Co-occurrence and association

Co-occurrence matrices capture which words appear together inside a context window. Common scores are pointwise mutual information (PMI) and conditional probability.

Scenario

The co-occurrence exam + leak + protest is a signal of a possible academic controversy — even if each word alone is common.

PMI (for revision)

PMI(x, y) = log [ P(x, y) / (P(x) P(y)) ]

High PMI means the pair occurs together more often than chance. New high-PMI pairs that were previously absent are novelty candidates.

3.6 What counts as a novel pattern?

Definition

A novel pattern is a statistically or semantically unexpected deviation from historical text distributions. It can appear as new term combinations, abrupt semantic shifts, or stylistic / sentiment anomalies.

Style often moves before the topic name is obvious. Normal news tone uses announced, discussed, reviewed. Crisis tone uses urgent, severe, emergency, casualties. Catch the style shift and you may catch the event earlier.

3.7 Frequency-based novelty

Treat each term’s frequency as a time series.

  • Sudden spikes → anomalies
  • Sustained increases → trends

Example: boycott appearing suddenly in entertainment news suggests a controversy.

3.8 Clustering, outliers, and topic drift

Cluster documents with TF–IDF vectors or embeddings. You learn dominant themes, minority clusters, and noise-versus-signal structure. Documents with high distance from centroids or low cluster membership are candidate novel events or threats — used in cyberbullying detection, misinformation spotting, and fraud alerts.

Topics are not static. They evolve, merge, and decay. Two standard tools:

  • dynamic topic models
  • sliding-window topic analysis

Example path: education → online classes → hybrid learning → AI tutoring.

What happensExample
Topic emergenceA new topic appears suddenly.A disaster topic that did not exist last month.
Topic driftAn existing topic changes vocabulary and meaning.Health drifting from hospitals → vaccines → boosters.

3.9 Mini case — news headline monitoring

Task: analyse daily headlines across months. Observation: sudden disaster vocabulary plus a coherent cluster of emergency-related news. Inference: an early crisis signal, possibly before official confirmation.

Solved essay C

Novel pattern or merely noise?

Problem as given in the lecture

You work for an Indian digital media-monitoring agency that tracks headlines and Twitter posts daily. For several weeks the dominant words are government, policy, meeting, development, economy.

Within 24 hours, exploratory analysis shows:

  • a sharp rise in protest, boycott, outrage, investigation
  • more short, urgent headlines than before
  • new co-occurrences leak + exam and scam + officials
  • a small document cluster that does not fit existing topics

Using data-exploration principles, explain how you would decide whether these observations are meaningful novel patterns or merely noise. Mention at least three exploratory techniques and justify each.

Model answer

I would not trust any one symptom. Noise can create a one-day word blip. A meaningful novel pattern should show up in frequency, structure, and semantics at the same time. I would use at least the three techniques below (a fourth is included because the lecture also highlights length and style).

1. Frequency-based novelty / time-series z-scores

Treat each new word as a daily count and compare today with the previous weeks. A sharp rise in protest, boycott, outrage, investigation is interesting only if it is large relative to the historical mean and spread (for example |z| > 2, the rule used in the next numerical). If the z-score is huge and the next day stays high, the burst is unlikely to be a random wobble. This technique answers: “Is the count statistically unexpected?”

2. Document-length and style distribution

The brief already notes shorter, more urgent headlines. Plot tokens per document and the crisis lexicon (urgent, breaking, outrage). Breaking news and coordinated alerts are typically shorter than policy explainers. A sudden left-shift in the length histogram, together with a style shift from meeting / development to crisis tone, is structural evidence, not just one lucky word. If length is unchanged and only one word ticked up, I would lean toward noise.

3. Co-occurrence / PMI of new pairs

leak + exam and scam + officials were previously absent. I would compute co-occurrence or PMI in a sliding window. Chance can raise one word; it is much less likely to create a brand-new, semantically coherent pair. High PMI on a pair that matches a real-world story (exam leak, official scam) is a strong novelty marker — the same logic as the lecture’s exam + leak + protest example.

4. Clustering and outlier distance (recommended fourth technique)

Vectorize recent documents (TF–IDF or embeddings) and cluster them against the previous weeks. A small cluster far from existing centroids, with low membership in “policy / economy” topics, is the lecture’s own signature of a novel event. If those outlier documents are near-duplicates, I also flag a collective anomaly (bots or a copy-paste campaign) rather than organic news.

Decision rule. Call it a meaningful novel pattern only if at least three of these agree: extreme frequency z-score, shorter/urgent style, new high-PMI pairs, and an outlier cluster. If only one word spikes and the cluster is empty, treat it as noise and keep watching. Either way, this is hypothesis generation — the lecture is explicit that ETDA does not replace a later model, it decides whether a model is even looking at the right event.

Question 2 · Z-score of D5

D5 frequency x = 32.

z = (x − μ) / σ = (32 − 3.5) / 1.12 = 28.5 / 1.12 ≈ 25.45

Using the exact root: (32 − 3.5) / √1.25 = 28.5 / 1.11803 ≈ 25.49.

Decision rule: anomaly if |z| > 2.

|25.45| > 2  ⇒  Day D5 is anomalous.

The value is not a borderline case. It is more than 25 standard deviations above the recent mean — far beyond ordinary variation.

Headlines did not just mention protest more often; they also became much shorter. In this module that is the signature of breaking-news / urgent alerts, not of ordinary policy copy. The nature of the text has changed: from longer informational headlines to short crisis headlines.

Question 4 · Novel pattern or random noise?

Conclusion: Days D5 and D6 represent a meaningful novel pattern, not random noise.

Numerical justification:

  1. Protest frequency jumps from a 2–5 band to 32 and then 36. zD5 ≈ 25.5, which fails the |z| ≤ 2 “normal” band by an enormous margin.
  2. The jump is not a one-day glitch. D6 stays extreme (36), so the series has switched regime — closer to a forming trend than to a single noisy outlier.
  3. Headline volume only rises from 120–130 to 135–140 (about +10%). The word protest rises from 3.5 to 32 (about +814%). So the signal is not “we simply collected more headlines.”
  4. Average length collapses from 14.5 to 7 and 6 (z ≈ −15 and −17). Two independent measurements — frequency and length — break at the same time. Random noise almost never moves both statistics this far, in a coherent breaking-news direction, for two days in a row.

This matches the lecture’s news-monitoring case: sudden crisis vocabulary plus short urgent headlines = early signal of an event, not a random flicker.

Chapter 4

Sentiment tracking and temporal analysis

4.1 What sentiment tracking is

Definition

Sentiment tracking analyses how emotions and opinions in text change over time. The goal is to identify shifts in public mood, reactions to events, and emotional escalation.

Typical applications: social-media monitoring, brand reputation, political opinion tracking.

4.2 Sentiment analysis fundamentals

Sentiment analysis is the computational technique that estimates the emotional tone of a text. The basic polarity classes are positive, negative, and neutral.

Example

Tweet: “The new metro line in Chennai is amazing!” → positive.

Modern models also detect discrete emotions: joy, anger, fear, sadness, surprise.

Example

“People are furious about the sudden exam cancellation.” → emotion = anger.

4.3 Methods

FamilyIdeaExamples from the lecture
Lexicon-based Look up words in a positive / negative dictionary. VADER, SentiWordNet
Classical machine learning Train a classifier on labelled text. Naïve Bayes, SVM, logistic regression
Deep learning Learn context and long-range tone from data. LSTM, BERT, other transformers

4.4 Tracking sentiment over time

  1. Collect text (tweets, headlines, reviews).
  2. Compute a sentiment score for each document.
  3. Aggregate scores by time interval (hour, day, week).
  4. Visualize the trend and look for shift points.

Cricket-match scenario: before the match, talk is positive about the team; during a loss, negative sentiment spikes; after official statements, sentiment stabilises. The point is not a single label — it is the dynamics of public reaction.

4.5 Visualizing sentiment

  • Line graphs — polarity over time; easy to see the moment of a shift.
  • Stacked area plots — share of positive (green), neutral (gray), negative (red).
  • Heat maps — intensity across topics or regions. Darker means stronger.

4.6 Sentiment anomalies

A sentiment anomaly is a dramatic, sudden change in emotional tone. Indicators:

  • a sharp negative spike
  • an unusual positive surge
  • rapid emotional escalation

Example: a flood of negative tweets about a product after a safety issue. Sentiment anomalies often mark controversies, crises, or coordinated campaigns — the emotional twin of the word-frequency anomalies in Chapter 1.

Chapter 5

Visual analytics, FutureLens, and scenario discovery

5.1 Visual analytics

Definition

Visual analytics combines computational text analysis with interactive visual interfaces so analysts can interpret large, complex corpora. The goal is to let humans explore patterns, anomalies, and relationships.

News, tweets, and blogs arrive too fast and in too many dimensions for a spreadsheet. Visual analytics helps people identify trends, detect anomalies, and explore relationships interactively.

Three components

  • Automated text mining — topic modelling, clustering, sentiment analysis.
  • Visualization tools — dashboards and interactive graphs.
  • Human interaction — analysts explore and interpret.

Example workflow

  1. Collect textual data (articles, tweets).
  2. Apply automated text-mining algorithms.
  3. Generate visual representations.
  4. Analysts interact with the views (zoom, filter, click a spike).
  5. Insights and decisions are derived.

Outcome: a better understanding of complex information streams than either a model or a chart alone.

5.2 FutureLens

Definition

FutureLens refers to analytical approaches that use trend detection, forecasting, and visual analysis to anticipate possible future events. It focuses on early signals in textual data: understand not only what is happening now, but what may happen next.

Electric-vehicle discussions in India — early signals might include:

  • increasing mentions of battery shortage
  • talk about charging infrastructure
  • policy announcements

A FutureLens reading of those signals may anticipate EV adoption friction and rising infrastructure demand.

5.3 Scenario discovery

Definition

Scenario discovery identifies possible future situations by analysing evolving textual narratives. It examines weak signals, emerging topics, and narrative shifts.

Weak signals are subtle patterns that appear before a major event: a small rise in posts about water shortages, early talk of disease symptoms, or a slow climb in complaints about infrastructure failures. They are not yet trends. They are the faint left edge of Figure 1.

Where it is used

  • Policy planning — governments reading public opinion.
  • Market forecasting — predicting consumer behaviour.
  • Disaster preparedness — early warnings in social media.
  • Strategic decisions — preparing organisations for uncertain futures.
Chapter takeaways
  • Visual analytics pairs machines with human interpretation.
  • FutureLens uses textual signals to anticipate what may come next.
  • Scenario discovery sketches possible futures from emerging narratives.
  • Together they support data-driven decisions in complex environments.
Chapter 6

Internet predation, cyberbullying, and current research

Large volumes of social-media communication create room for harmful behaviour. Two threats studied with text mining are internet predation and cyberbullying. The goal is to detect harmful communication patterns automatically and protect vulnerable users.

6.1 Internet predation

Definition

Internet predation is online behaviour in which someone exploits or manipulates vulnerable users through deceptive communication. Common targets include children, teenagers, and socially vulnerable people. Typical platforms: social media, games, and chat apps.

Predators often show a recognisable linguistic and behavioural sequence:

  • gradual trust-building language
  • requests for personal information
  • emotional manipulation
  • rapid escalation of the conversation
Example phrases from the lecture

“Don’t tell anyone about our chats.” · “Let’s move to a private platform.”

6.2 Cyberbullying

Definition

Cyberbullying is repeated aggressive behaviour through digital channels. Forms include harassment, public humiliation, threats, and spreading rumours. Common platforms named in the lecture: Instagram, Twitter, WhatsApp, and gaming chats.

Linguistic indicators:

  • abusive vocabulary
  • insults and threats
  • repeated negative sentiment
  • sarcastic or humiliating expressions

Example message: “You are useless and everyone hates you.”

6.3 Text-mining techniques for detection

TechniqueWhat it catches
Lexical analysisAbusive or offensive words.
Sentiment analysisNegative emotional tone.
Emotion detectionAnger, hostility, hate speech.
Temporal pattern analysisRepeated abuse, escalation, continuous targeting of one user.
Network analysisClusters of attacking accounts, coordinated campaigns, troll networks.

Temporal example: many hostile comments aimed at the same user in a short interval. Network view: nodes are users, edges are interactions. A star around one victim, or a dense attacking clique, is a collective anomaly — the same idea as the fake-review swarm, now applied to harm.

6.5 Ethical and social considerations

Detecting harmful communication creates its own risks. The lecture lists four challenges:

  • protecting user privacy
  • avoiding algorithmic bias
  • ensuring fairness in detection
  • transparency in automated decisions

Responsible systems have to balance safety with individual rights. A high recall detector that flags every angry joke as predation is not a success.

Solved numerical E

Cyberbullying monitor — “twice the average” rule

Problem as given on the last slide

A text-mining system monitors social-media discussion of a new mobile game to detect possible cyberbullying trends. Negative-sentiment posts for the first four days: 100, 110, 90, 120. On the fifth day the system records 210 negative posts.

Rule: a value is an anomaly only if it exceeds twice the average of the previous observations.

Question: based on this rule, which statement is correct?

The slide does not print the multiple-choice options. The calculation below is enough to pick the right option in an exam: compare 210 with the threshold and apply the word exceeds strictly.

Full working

Step 1. Average of the previous four observations.

μ = (100 + 110 + 90 + 120) / 4 = 420 / 4 = 105

Step 2. Twice that average is the threshold.

T = 2μ = 2 × 105 = 210

Step 3. Compare day 5 with the threshold.

x₅ = 210  T = 210  x₅ > T → 210 > 210 → False

The rule says “exceeds,” which in mathematics means strictly greater than, not “greater than or equal to.” Day 5 is exactly equal to twice the previous average, so it does not qualify as an anomaly under the stated rule.

Correct conclusion:

  • Day 5 is not an anomaly according to this system.
  • The series is still worrying in real life (a jump from ~105 to 210 is a doubling), but the programmed rule is mute on equality.

If an option says “210 is an anomaly because it is twice the mean,” that option is wrong under a strict reading of “exceeds.” If an option says “210 is not an anomaly because it does not exceed 210,” that option is right.

Exam trap — do not mix rules

This is not the z-score rule from Numerical D. You do not need σ here. If you wrongly compute a z-score you will invent a different answer. Always use the rule printed in the question.

For curiosity only: population SD of {100, 110, 90, 120} is √[ (25 + 25 + 225 + 225) / 4 ] = √125 ≈ 11.18, so z = (210 − 105) / 11.18 ≈ 9.4, which would be anomalous under |z| > 2. Different rule, different decision. That contrast is a good exam sentence.
